1 · Pressure

Pressure is force per area, P = F / A. Gas pressure comes from molecules colliding with the walls of their container.

Reading an open-end manometer. Mercury is pushed away from the side with the higher pressure.

  • Open arm higher → the gas pushes harder than the air: Pgas = Patm + h
  • Flask arm higher → the air pushes harder than the gas: Pgas = Patm − h

Worked example (slide 6): Patm = 760 mmHg, open arm 120 mm higher → Pgas = 880 mmHg × (14.70 psi / 760 mmHg) = 17.0 psi.

2 · One equation

Everything in this unit comes from the ideal gas law:

PV = nRT

History runs the other way: the individual laws were measured first, and Clapeyron combined them into one equation in 1834. Kinetic theory (section 5) later derived the same equation from Newton's laws. So the named laws are the evidence; PV = nRT is the summary.

Always use kelvin. The proportionalities hold only on an absolute scale: K = °C + 273.15. Pressures and volumes may be in any unit, as long as both states use the same one.

3 · Using PV = nRT for one state

Rearrange for whatever is unknown: P = nRT/V V = nRT/P n = PV/(RT) T = PV/(nR)

Before substituting, put every value in R's units: atm, L, mol, K.

  • STP is 0 °C (273.15 K) and 1 atm. One mole of an ideal gas at STP occupies 22.4 L. At 25 °C and 1 atm it occupies 24.5 L.
  • Molar mass: find n = PV/(RT), then M = mass ÷ n. In one step, M = mRT/(PV).
  • Density: substitute n = m/M into PV = nRT and you get d = m/V = PM/(RT), or M = dRT/P.

4 · Gas stoichiometry

The balanced equation counts moles, so every problem runs through moles. PV = nRT is the bridge between a gas's volume and its moles.

grams of A → moles of A → (mole ratio) → moles of B → V of B = nRT/P

The mole ratio comes from the coefficients: moles of B = moles of A × (coefficient of B ÷ coefficient of A).

Gas to gas at the same T and P: because V ∝ n (Avogadro), gas volumes combine in the same ratio as the coefficients, and R is not needed. For N2 + 3 H2 → 2 NH3, 15.0 L of H2 makes 15.0 × (2/3) = 10.0 L of NH3.

5 · Dalton's law of partial pressures

Each gas in a mixture behaves as if it were alone:

Ptotal = P1 + P2 + … Pi = χi Ptotal, χi = ni/ntotal

Collecting a gas over water. The bottle holds the gas plus water vapor, so Pgas = Ptotal − PH₂O. Use Pgas in PV = nRT.

6 · Kinetic theory

Gas particles are tiny compared with the space between them, move randomly, and collide elastically. Averaging their collisions with the walls gives, for one mole, PV = ⅔ N0 εavg (slide 19), which matches PV = nRT only if the average kinetic energy is proportional to the kelvin temperature:

KEavg = (3/2) RT per mole urms = √(3RT/M)

For speeds, use R = 8.314 J/(mol·K) and M in kg/mol, because 1 J = 1 kg·m²/s².

Graham's law of effusion: lighter gases are faster: rateA / rateB = √(MB / MA)

7 · Real gases

Real gases depart from ideal behavior at high pressure and low temperature, for two reasons the ideal model ignores (slide 23):

  • Particle volume (b): particles take up space, so they have less room to move: V becomes V − nb. This raises the pressure.
  • Attractions (a): neighbors pull each particle back from the wall, so it hits less hard. This lowers the pressure by a·n²/V².

[P + a(n/V)²](V − nb) = nRT → P = nRT/(V − nb) − a·n²/V²

The compressibility factor Z = PV/(nRT) is 1 for an ideal gas. Z < 1 means attractions win; Z > 1 means particle volume wins.

The Boyle temperature TB = a/(bR) is where the two effects balance, so the gas behaves ideally over the widest range of pressure.

8 · Common mistakes

  • Using °C instead of kelvin (or adding 273 to a temperature change).
  • Using R = 8.314 with atm and liters, or 0.08206 with kPa.
  • Leaving volume in mL, or pressure in mmHg, when R wants L and atm.
  • Two states with mixed units: P1 in mmHg and P2 in atm.
  • Ratio upside down. Predict first: should the answer go up or down?
  • Forgetting to subtract water's vapor pressure for a gas collected over water.
  • Using g/mol instead of kg/mol in urms; flipping Graham's ratio.
  • Stoichiometry: skipping the mole ratio, or writing it upside down.
  • Van der Waals: forgetting to square n and V in a·n²/V², or using V − b instead of V − nb.
